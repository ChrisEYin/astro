There's a concept called the [optimal stopping problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_stopping). I originally read about this in a book called [Algorithms To Live By](https://www.amazon.sg/dp/1627790365).

The point is -- when you are considering a set of options, when you should you pick a path forward vs. continue to search/weigh different options?

The book uses a shopping mall's parking lot as an example. If you want to find the closest spot to the entrance, how long do you circle around before you pick? If you pick too soon, you might see a better spot later. But if you circle too long, you might have also passed on good spots that would've been fine. So when do you choose?

To cut to it -- the answer is 37%. There is a bunch of math explaining why 37% is the number, but let's take it as fact. Which means the optimal time to stop searching is after you have seen 37% of the options. So after you cross the 37% threshold, the next best one that you see compared to all of the earlier options, you take.

So in the mall example, if you've allocated 10 minutes to search for parking than after about 3.5 minutes, you just pick the first spot that is better than the ones you saw previously.

Most meaningful problems in life have no fixed outcomes. And so you see this a lot when people are presented with nebulous problems to solve. They got bogged down thinking about every possible problem, all the different scenarios, etc. And while it's good to think about things, most of the time it's better just to act.

The example that is used in the book is around looking for the right parking spot in mall. If you take a parking spot too early, you may find another one after that is closer to the mall. But if you are constantly waiting for the perfect spot, you probably spend too much time waiting and miss what would've been good spots.

There is some math as to why this is the answer, the the short answer is -- 37%. Meaning the optimal time to stop is after you have observed what you believe to be 37% of the landscape. If you use the parking lot example, this means once you've seen roughly 37% of what you think are the available opportunities, then after that you just pick the next best one that you see.

Most things in life are a question of the optimal stopping problem. For example in work, lots of things are unclear how far you should go before you do something. Meaning -- if you are writing a proposal, you can wait until you know every single thing about it until you know the answer. So you spend lots of time writing the proposal, instead of going forward and just trying to do something. This kills time, and ultimately slows you down the most out of everything.

On the other end, if you go too quickly and you have no idea what you are doing, then you are probably not thinking about this enough.

I find the simple heuristic is to always wait, give it some time to think, and then finally you want to figure out what you are doing on top of it and set a deadline in which you will act after. For most things that are not one way doors, you should probably just act right away. For things that are one way doors, you should stop, let it marinate, digest the problem, and then try to determine the right answer.

Progress is ultimately the most important thing and so you should primarily focus on that.

called the optimal stopping problem. The idea is very simple. If you are looking for parking at a mall, how do you choose the best spot? If you pick too early, you might be far away and see a better spot pop up as you're parking. On the other end, if you circle around too long you probably have spent too much time looking and are wasting time.

There are many names this goes by, including the secretary problem, etc. There is some complicated math that I do not understand so I'll have to take the word of author, but the answer is 37%. If you can do a rough sizing of the entire pool of opportunities, after you have explored 37% of them then you should pick the next best one. And so that's how you decide on the best thing.

And you can apply this idea to anything. In startups, the easiest way to think about this is how much work to do on a task. If you think about a task, there are 2 ends of a spectrum. You can spend all the time in the world apeing random ideas without any thought. On the other end, you can also spend all your time in the world researching and planning without ever doing. And my belief has always been that at the end of the day, hard works beats intelligence. Meaning -- just pick up the phone. And so the inclindation is to just do the work rather than to think about the work.

And so when you are thinking about startups, ideas, managing a team, etc. as you scale, you first start off with pure intuition and the intellectual horsepower and intensity of the founders. Oftentimes as the founders, they have very good intuition on the market in front of them and so what appears to be just apeing with no research is actually just the well worn experiene showing its head. Not to say that there aren't any mistakes, but if you get your startup to a point where you are scaling and thinking about this, than generally speaking your intuition has gone well.

But the team always mimics what you do. And so they can begin to mimic the same decisions. And when you are going 100 miles an hour, you don't have time to watch everyone and make sure they are doing everything correctly. You are hiring aggressively often without process, criteria, thought, etc and you are working through it. And so the as you are rapidly adding people, people then mimic the teams behavior and start doing things without thinking.

But that's a death sentence. As that happens, the intuition that you have as a founder has likely not rubbed off on the team yet. And so while they may or may not be making good decisions, not only do they not have the intuition but they also likely don't have access to the full set of information that you might have. And so then poor decisions get made.

Inevitably there is some kind of pause and reset that comes up. When that cleanup happens and you go back and revisit all the bad decisions that were made, a statis comes over the organization. As trust and authority change and and people are no longer empowered to make decisions or feel afraid about things, they revert to the other side of the spectrum -- trying to ensure that everything is 100% thought through before they act.

This is a mistake because you don't yet have the scale to justify this slowness (ie distribution as a core strength is not yours yet) and then on the other side you are also still moving too slowly. And so in this moment you need to break the team down from this and first ensure you have the right people in place, the right goals are in mind, the right process in place in order to measure progress against goals progressively as opposed to ex-post. And once you have these things in place, then we need to make sure that the team breaks away from their mental model of doing everything perfect and go back to 37%.
